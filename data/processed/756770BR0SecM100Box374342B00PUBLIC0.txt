Annual Report to the Executive Directors on the Special Programme for 
Research and Training in Tropical Diseases (TDR) 
1. This is the second annual report to the Executive Directors. It 
will concentrate on current developments in scientific output, shortage of 
funds, and the evolving role of the Bank. 
History of TDR 
2. The history of TDR was traced in the first annual report to the 
Executive Directors in July 1982. In brief, the Programme was founded in 
1977 by WHO to attract funds and scientific effort to the solution of 
outstanding problems in the control of the six major tropical diseases: 
malaria, schistosomiasis, leishmaniasis, filariasis, trypanosomiasis and 
leprosy. There is a particular focus on the problems of sub-Saharan 
Africa. The ultimate objective is to produce effective and acceptable 
tools to control these diseases and to do so by two lines of action: by 
direct support of fundamental and applied research and by strengthening 
research capability in endemic countries through institution building and 
training. Each disease constitutes a public health problem for which there 
is no control technology currently available to the tropical developing 
countries which is simple and cheap enough in application. Research on 
these diseases had been largely neglected by the scientific community and 
the pharmaceutical industry, but the scientific techniques in immunology 
and molecular biology developed to combat the diseases of the 
industrialized countries are potentially powerful new tools in the battle 
against these developing country scourges. 
3. The Bank became a non-contributing sponsor (with WHO and UNDP) of 
TDR in 1977. Our support was considered to be important in gaining the 
commitment and participation of multilateral and bilateral donors and the 
involvement of developing countries. Substantial, assured, long-term 
support is needed to stimulate promising lines of research by the world's 
health science research community, to coordinate succeeding experimental 
and trial stages, and to prepare developing countries to adapt technology 
and implement programs. The Bank's involvement in TDR complements its 
health lending which is directed to expanding and strengthening basic 
health services to bring available technology to people who presently have 
no access to such services. 
4. Apart from establishing the confidence of the donor community 
through its support of TDR, the Bank's role has been as a sponsor (and 
member of the Joint Coordinating Board (JCB) and its Standing Committee) to 
influence the management of the Programme using its experience in the 
operation and management of development activities; WHO as Executing Agency 
has been very responsive to such guidance, which in the formative days of 
TDR led to substantive improvements in its organization. Lastly, the Bank 
has provided financial management for a fund through which many of the 
donors prefer to channel their TDR contributions. Although initially the 
Bank did not itself contribute funds to TDR, in 1981 it recognized that its 
role would be enhanced if it did so and it decided in that year to 
contribute US$2.48 million, which was 10% of the total contributions 
expected in that calendar year. The figure of 10% was based on the level 
of Bank participation in CCIAR and the Onchocerciasis Control Programme.

5. In 1980 a major (quinquennial) review of the Programme was made 
by an independent team of experts. The report of that body concluded that 
the Programme was effective, well targetted, and well managed both 
scientifically and administratively and that it was making major 
contributions towards the solution of outstanding problems in tropical 
disease control. At its July 1982 annual meeting, the JCB of TDR accepted 
the report and adopted its recommendations to streamline research 
management and to consolidate administration. 
Recent scientific progress in the Programme of Research and Development 
TDR has invested heavily in applying new techniques in cellular 
biology and social science to fundamental problems of parasite, vector and 
host. This investment is now paying off, and the past year has seen a 
sharp increase in the publication of research findings, each making some 
advance towards disease control. The most striking advances are in 
immunology, leading to the possibility of vaccines, parasite metabolism 
leading to new drugs, and vector biology leading to possibilities of new 
control mechanisms. The number of leads now available are more than the 
Programme can follow, and therein lies its main immediate problem of 
obtaining enough funds to sustain its momentum. 
Although research progress has been made in all the six diseases, 
that in malaria and leprosy is perhaps the most striking: 
Malaria has increased since 1979, and it is clear that control 
programs will not eradicate it. Moreover, both parasite and mosquito are 
becoming resistant to standard drugs and insecticide. TDR has led much of 
the research that now promises new solutions. A few years ago, a vaccine 
for malaria seemed barely feasible, but work supported by TDR has now 
brought the possibility of a vaccine very close to reality, to the stage of 
producing pure antigen by modern techniques of cell biology. A number of 
major research institutions with strong financial support have now joined 
TDR to take this research through the next, expensive development stage, 
and so to completion and general application. 
To combat growing drug resistance TDR has taken a lead in 
stimulating research institutes and industry to develop a major new drug, 
mefloquine, and to work on derivatives of an old Chinese drug, Qinghaosu. 
Resistance to mefloquine could also arise, and Hoffman-Laroche, who will 
start distributing the drug in 1983, have offered, in the public interest, 
to keep its release within guidelines agreed with WHO which are designed 
to delay as long as practical the build-up of resistance; this agreement 
represents the first such global action with respect to a new drug. 
To deal with resistance of mosquitoes to DDT, TDR has advanced 
the development of alternative methods of vector control, especially the 
use of bacteria toxic to larvae B. Thuringiensis (now being applied 
operationally in vector control in the Onchocerciasis Control Programme in 
West Africa) and B. Sphaericus, which is now under early field trial. To 
pursue vector control more firmly, TDR will set up a program of research 
into basic vector biology.

Leprosy research has made good progress in developing new drugs, 
understanding the mechanisms of drug resistance, establishing methods of 
epidemiological study and field trials. But the most encouraging progress 
is in the preparation of the first batch of killed bacteria suitable for 
trial as a vaccine. Trials will start next year and, because of the slow 
rate of development of leprosy, will need at least ten years to complete, 
one fact that highlights the need for consistent and predictable funding 
support. 
Recent Progress in the Programme to Strengthen Research Capability 
A major objective of TDR is to strengthen research and training 
capability within endemic countries, and one-third of its funds have been 
committed in this direction. As a measure of its success, 21 of 32 
institutions that have received long-term support now receive competitive 
grants from the Scientific Research Programme; one-third of all papers in 
the Sixth Report came from scientists in the developing countries; and 35 
of the 113 scientists who were awarded training support received all or 
part of that training in another developing country. TDR now supports 
eight M.Sc. courses in developing countries. 
Financial Support for TDR 
Now that the scientific program is manifestly productive and on a 
firm footing, the main determinant of progress will be the adequacy of 
funding. Donor support has not increased as projected, contributions 
having levelled off since 1979, thus curtailing the expansion of the 
Programme that had been planned. There is now the prospect of important 
scientific results having to sit on the shelf because funding is not 
available for their development. The table shows this levelling off of

In 1981, the JCB projected a budget for the 1984-85 biennium of 
US$70.6 million but, taking into account the probable shortfall of funds, 
the biennial budget actually recommended by the Scientific and Technical 
Advisory Committee (STAC) for JCB approval in July 1983 was $66.7 million. 
Actual pledges so far for 1984-85 amount only to approximately $58 million 
despite the enthusiastic endorsement of the Programme by nearly all members 
and observers at the recent JCB. This shortfall of funds comes at a 
particularly unfortunate time, for the programme is now facing major 
challenges which compete for TDR support. These challenges are 
consequences of the progress made by TDR so far, and include field trials 
of vaccines and drugs and the follow-up of new discoveries. Field trials 
are a particularly costly phase; as an example, trials for leprosy vaccine

Future Challenges for TDR 
Whilst the broad objective remains the identification of 
solutions to the six chosen diseases, the immediate challenges are: 
1. To focus research support on the most promising leads. Here 
there is an embarrassment of promising leads and increased support for one 
activity can only curtail another. STAC has proposed increased support for 
malaria, leprosy, and social and economic research, a main object of this 
last being to attract economists to the study of tropical disease. TDR 
management has also to ensure that the balance of the program is maintained 
when funding problems force cuts; 
2. To set up and fund expensive large scale field trials for 
vaccines and drugs; 
3. To consolidate research capability in endemic countries and to 
transfer responsibility to national governments; 
4. To transfer new technologies to national health services; 
5. To strengthen co-operation with other institutions and funding 
agencies and with industry (already there are co-operative programs with 41 
industries in 10 states); and 
6. To attract increased financial contributions. 
Fund raising and the role of the Bank 
The Bank's initial decision to help fund TDR was taken both in 
recognition of the intrinsic worth of the programme and as an encouragement 
to other donors. The Bank also recognizes that TDR's own funding has a 
multiplier effect by directing the resources of research institutions and 
other funding agencies towards tropical diseases. 
The Bank's contribution to TDR since FY82 has been at the rate of 
about 10% of total contributions; in FY84 it has agreed to contribute 12% 
up to a maximum of $3.2 million. It has been agreed among the 
Co-sponsoring Agencies that they should take joint action in attracting 
contributions and detailed targets and strategies are now to be worked out. 
In addition to its direct financial contribution, the Bank hopes 
to support to a greater extent than hitherto TDR's research activities 
involving economic and behavioral issues where the Bank has a comparative 
advantage. Priority areas appear to include further analysis into the 
economic and human costs of tropical diseases, in order to improve 
judgements about the importance of tropical disease research generally and
